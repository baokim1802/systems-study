# Design a Payment System

> Most systems can shrug off a rare duplicate or a lost event. A payment system can't: charge a customer twice and you've broken trust; lose a record and the books don't balance. The scale is usually modest. The real challenge is correctness when networks time out halfway through moving money.

## The big idea

Think of an old-fashioned shop with a strict bookkeeper. Every time money moves, she writes it in a ledger **twice**: once as money leaving one account, once as money arriving in another. "Customer paid $100: cash drawer +$100, sales +$100." If the two columns ever disagree, she knows instantly that something is wrong. She never erases a line; mistakes are fixed with a new correcting line. And every night she compares her ledger with the bank's statement, line by line.

That's how real payment systems work:

- **Double-entry ledger:** every movement is recorded as balanced debits and credits.
- **Append-only:** entries are never updated or deleted, only reversed.
- **Reconciliation:** regularly compare your records with the bank's or payment provider's.

And because a customer may double-click "Pay" or a request may time out, every step must be **idempotent** (Day 43): doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once.

## Who's involved

| Party | Role |
|---|---|
| Customer | pays with a card or wallet |
| Merchant (us) | an e-commerce marketplace |
| **PSP** (payment service provider: Stripe, Adyen, PayPal) | talks to card networks for us |
| Card networks (Visa, Mastercard) and banks | actually move the money |

We almost never touch raw card numbers. Handling them pulls you into **PCI DSS**, a strict security standard. Instead, the PSP's hosted form or SDK collects the card and gives us a **token** that stands for it.

## Step 1: Clarify requirements

**Functional**

- Customers pay for orders (pay-in); we pay sellers later (pay-out), minus a platform fee.
- Refunds, full or partial.
- Payment status visible to customers and support.
- Daily reconciliation with the PSP's settlement reports.

**Non-functional**

- **Never double-charge, never lose a payment.** Correctness beats latency.
- Auditable: who moved what, when, and why, forever.
- Highly available checkout, but it's better to fail a payment cleanly than to guess.
- Security: no card data in our systems; encrypted, access-controlled financial data.

## Step 2: Back-of-the-envelope estimates

```text
Transactions/day         10M
Average TPS              10e6 / 86,400 ≈ 116 transactions/s
Peak (Black Friday ×10)  ≈ 1,200 TPS
Ledger entries           ~3 per transaction (customer, seller, fee) → 30M rows/day
Row size                 ~200 B → 6 GB/day → ~2.2 TB/year
```

Compare that with chat (Day 58: ~231,000 messages/s). Payments are a **small-QPS, high-correctness** problem. A well-run relational database (Day 20) with ACID transactions (Day 22) handles this comfortably, sharded by merchant or account if it grows. Don't reach for an eventually consistent store just because it scales.

## Step 3: API

```text
POST /v1/payments
Idempotency-Key: 6f1c2a9e-checkout-order-5512
{ "orderId": "5512", "amountMinor": 4999, "currency": "USD", "paymentMethodToken": "pm_tok_..." }
→ 201 { "paymentId": "pay_81", "status": "PENDING" }

GET  /v1/payments/{id}                      status
POST /v1/payments/{id}/refunds              { amountMinor, Idempotency-Key }
POST /v1/webhooks/psp                       PSP tells us what happened (signed)
```

Two rules hide in that request:

- **Money is an integer in minor units** (cents): `4999` means $49.99. Remember Day 2: `0.1 + 0.2 !== 0.3` in floating point. Never use floats for money.
- **An idempotency key** generated by the client once per checkout attempt. Retries reuse the same key.

## Step 4: Data model

```text
payments        (payment_id PK, idempotency_key UNIQUE, order_id, amount_minor, currency,
                 status, psp_reference, created_at, updated_at, version)
payment_events  (payment_id, seq, from_status, to_status, payload, at)     append-only audit
accounts        (account_id, owner, type: asset|liability|revenue, currency)
ledger_entries  (entry_id, txn_id, account_id, direction: debit|credit, amount_minor, created_at)
                append-only; for each txn_id: sum(debits) == sum(credits)
outbox          (id, topic, payload, published_at)                         Day 44
```

Balances are **derived**: `balance = sum(credits) - sum(debits)` for an account (or the reverse, depending on account type), cached in a balance table updated in the same transaction.

## Step 6: Deep dives

### The payment state machine

```text
CREATED ─▶ PENDING (sent to PSP) ─▶ SUCCEEDED ─▶ (PARTIALLY_)REFUNDED
                     │
                     ├─▶ FAILED
                     └─▶ UNKNOWN (timeout) ──query PSP──▶ SUCCEEDED / FAILED
```

Each transition is a conditional update (`WHERE status = 'PENDING' AND version = 3`) so duplicate webhooks or retries can't apply twice. Cards often use **authorize then capture**: authorization reserves the money on the card when you order, capture actually takes it when the item ships.

### Idempotency, end to end

```text
1. Client sends POST with Idempotency-Key K.
2. Server: INSERT payments (..., idempotency_key = K)   ← unique index
     - inserted?      → new payment, continue
     - duplicate key? → return the stored result for K; do nothing else
3. Server calls the PSP passing a key derived from K (PSPs such as Stripe accept idempotency keys)
4. Store the PSP result with the payment; respond.
```

So a double-click, a client retry and a server retry all collapse into one charge. "Exactly-once money movement" is really **at-least-once attempts + idempotency at every hop**.

### The scary case: timeouts

We call the PSP and after 30 s get no answer. Did the charge happen? **We don't know.** Rules:

- Never mark it FAILED and never retry with a **new** key; that's how double charges happen.
- Mark it `UNKNOWN`, then **ask the PSP** (query by our reference) or retry with the **same** key, which the PSP dedups.
- The PSP's webhook will eventually tell us too. Webhooks can arrive late, twice, or out of order, so handle them idempotently and verify their signatures.

### Double-entry bookkeeping

A customer pays $100.00 for an item; the platform keeps a 10% fee. One ledger transaction:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| PSP receivable (money the PSP owes us) | 10,000 | |
| Seller payable (we owe the seller) | | 9,000 |
| Platform fee revenue | | 1,000 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **10,000** |

Debits equal credits, always. If a bug writes only one side, the books stop balancing and alarms go off. A refund is a **new** transaction that reverses the entries; nothing is edited.

```js
// Validate a ledger transaction and compute balances. Amounts are integer cents.
function isBalanced(entries) {
  let debits = 0, credits = 0;
  for (const e of entries) {
    if (!Number.isInteger(e.amount) || e.amount <= 0) return false;
    if (e.dir === "debit") debits += e.amount; else credits += e.amount;
  }
  return entries.length >= 2 && debits === credits;
}

function balances(entries) {            // credit-normal view: credits add, debits subtract
  const b = {};
  for (const e of entries) b[e.account] = (b[e.account] ?? 0) + (e.dir === "credit" ? e.amount : -e.amount);
  return b;
}

const sale = [
  { account: "psp_receivable", dir: "debit", amount: 10000 },
  { account: "seller_payable", dir: "credit", amount: 9000 },
  { account: "fee_revenue", dir: "credit", amount: 1000 },
];
console.log(isBalanced(sale));   // true
console.log(balances(sale));     // { psp_receivable: -10000, seller_payable: 9000, fee_revenue: 1000 }
```

### Splitting money without losing a cent

Split $100.00 three ways and `10000 / 3 = 3333.33…`. You can't pay a third of a cent. Use integer division and hand out the remainder deterministically: `3334 + 3333 + 3333 = 10000`. The total must always be exact.

### Reconciliation

Every day the PSP sends a **settlement report**: every transaction it processed, fees, and the amount it will deposit. A reconciliation job matches it against our ledger by PSP reference and amount. Typical mismatches:

- In the PSP report but not in our ledger: a webhook was lost, or we crashed after charging. Fix by recording it.
- In our ledger but not the PSP's: we recorded success wrongly. Investigate.
- Amount or currency differs, or fees differ from the contract.

Mismatches go to a queue for automatic fixes or a human finance team. Reconciliation is the **safety net** that catches every bug the rest of the design missed.

### Coordinating with orders and inventory

Payment, order and inventory live in different services, so there is no single database transaction. Use a **saga** (Day 44): reserve inventory → charge → confirm order; if the charge fails, release the reservation (a compensating action). Use the **outbox** to publish "payment succeeded" exactly when the DB commit happens.

## Step 7: Bottlenecks and trade-offs

- **Consistency over availability** (Day 39): if the ledger DB can't confirm a write, fail the payment rather than guess.
- **Synchronous vs async confirmation:** show "processing" and confirm via webhook instead of holding the user's request open.
- **Hot accounts:** the platform fee account gets an entry in every transaction; shard its balance into sub-accounts or compute it in batches.
- **Multiple PSPs** improve availability and fees but multiply reconciliation work.

## In an interview

Interviewers listen for: integer minor units, idempotency keys, a clear state machine with an UNKNOWN state for timeouts, an immutable double-entry ledger, reconciliation, and no raw card data. Model summary:

> "Checkout tokenizes the card with the PSP, then calls our payment service with an idempotency key. We insert the payment with a unique key, call the PSP with the same key, and move through PENDING to SUCCEEDED or FAILED via conditional updates, treating timeouts as UNKNOWN and resolving them by querying the PSP or retrying with the same key. On success we write balanced, append-only double-entry ledger rows and an outbox event in one ACID transaction. A nightly job reconciles our ledger against the PSP's settlement file. It's only ~1,000 TPS at peak, so we optimize for correctness with a relational database."

## Common mistakes

- **Floats for money.** Use integer minor units (or a decimal type) everywhere.
- **Retrying a timed-out charge with a new key.** That's a double charge.
- **Updating balances in place without a ledger.** You lose the audit trail and can't find bugs.
- **Trusting webhooks blindly.** Verify signatures; handle duplicates and out-of-order delivery.
- **Storing card numbers.** Let the PSP tokenize them.
- **Over-engineering for scale.** 1,000 TPS doesn't need an eventually consistent NoSQL ledger.
